01 — Executive recommendation

Rename, narrow, and go embarrassingly deep on one thing.

You have a genuinely good wedge and a stack that fits it. The risk is not ambition — it's breadth. This brief, taken literally, describes a 2-year platform. Build that in MVP and you will ship something mediocre on every axis. The win is to pick the single hardest, most valuable job — multi-event weddings where different guests are invited to different ceremonies — and make that feel category-best, then let one clean data model carry you into birthdays and corporate later.

Rename to Occasio, ship one breathtaking template, charge once per event, and own South Asian multi-event weddings before touching anything else.

Where I push back on the brief
  • CUT18 registration models in MVP. Ship one (per-household token). Design the rest into the schema; build on demand.
  • CUTGenerous freemium. Free tier = private draft + preview only. Publishing and sending are paid, or you'll drown in tire-kickers and support.
  • CUTMany templates at launch. One, exceptional. Template breadth is a Phase-2 growth lever, not an MVP requirement.

  • HIDECorporate language in the UI. Keep it in the data model, out of the surface, until you actually sell to corporates.
The one risk that matters most

Premium is binary. If the first template and the RSVP flow don't feel category-best on a phone, nothing else in this document matters.

Build those two surfaces to a magazine standard before you build anything else. Everything in §22–§24 is sized around that bar.

02 — Brand & naming

A name that can hold a wedding — and everything after it.

A brand name has one job at this stage: be sayable, ownable, and big enough to grow into. It should make a couple proud to put it at the bottom of their wedding website, and make a corporate buyer comfortable three years later. Measured that way, the working name fails — and a clear winner emerges.

Verdict on "Simvites" — rename
It caps you at "invites"
The name literally is the smallest feature. It can't stretch to event sites, guest ops, or non-wedding occasions without feeling wrong.
It reads cheap
"Sim-" evokes SIM cards and "simple"; "-vites" is a fragment. Together it sounds like a budget app, not a brand you'd proudly share.
It mis-types and mis-hears
Simvites / Simvights / Symvites — real spelling and pronunciation friction every time it's spoken aloud at a wedding.
It's weakly distinctive
A portmanteau of a generic term in a crowded "invite" namespace — harder to trademark, harder to rank, easy to confuse.

Investor

04 — Target users & personas

One buyer. Many anxious stakeholders.

The couple pays, but they are rarely alone — a mother runs one side's list, a planner watches over everything, and hundreds of guests (some 75, some 7) touch the product once. Design for the buyer's confidence and the guest's ease; everything else follows.

Pays when

05 — Product principles

Fifteen rules that settle arguments.

North star

Make hosts look effortlessly tasteful, and make every guest feel personally invited.

01
Taste is the product
If a control lets a user make something ugly, don't ship that control. Curate, don't expose.
02
The guest is the volume user
The phone RSVP is used 100× more than the editor. Optimise it above everything.
03
No spreadsheets, ever
Every guest fact lives in one model. Export is a feature — never the place truth actually lives.
04
Logic over forms
Per-event, per-guest invitation rules are first-class — and enforced server-side, not in the UI.
05
Separate fragile from safe
Guest ops can't break the design; editing can't leak a draft. Different rooms, different keys.
06
Fluency, not decoration
Offer cultural presets; default to neutral elegance; never assume or impose a religion.
07
Guests are not users
No accounts, no passwords. Secure token links, and nothing stored that could ever leak a list.
08
Mobile-first, truly
Design at 375px first. Desktop is the enhancement, not the canvas everything is drawn on.
09
Calm under pressure
This runs during the most stressful week of a life. Reliability beats cleverness, always.
10
Accessible by default
AA contrast, large targets, real focus states, type an 80-year-old can read on a phone.
11
Fast is a feature
Public sites read from static published snapshots. Sub-second loads on a hotel Wi-Fi.
12
Buildable by one founder + AI
Prefer managed services and boring, well-trodden patterns over anything clever or bespoke.
13
Broad nouns, narrow UI
Schema speaks site/event/guest; the surface speaks the host's words (couple, ceremony, mehndi).
14
Flexible by structure
Hosts add, rename, reorder, hide and duplicate events freely. Nothing about the day is hard-coded.
15
Publishing is deliberate
Drafts stay private. Going live and notifying guests are explicit, considered, separate choices.
06 — South Asian coverage strategy

"South Asian" is not one wedding. So don't build modes — build primitives.

A Punjabi Sikh wedding, a Tamil Hindu wedding, a Pakistani Nikah and a London interfaith civil ceremony share almost nothing in structure — and everything in needing to look beautiful and tell guests what to do. The trap is building a "Hindu mode" and a "Sikh mode." The win is a flexible event engine plus thoughtful presets a host opts into and then edits freely.

Mechanism 01
Neutral by default
Every new site starts elegant and culture-neutral. No symbols, no assumptions — a blank, beautiful canvas.
Mechanism 02
Opt-in preset packs
A host can apply a "Sikh wedding" or "Gujarati Hindu" preset that seeds suggested events, copy and notes — as a starting point.
Mechanism 03
Everything is editable
A preset is a head-start, never a cage. Rename, remove, reorder, add — including events we've never heard of.
Mechanism 04
Custom types & labels
"Create your own event," rename "Couple" to anything, relabel "RSVP." The schema bends to the host's language.

08 — Religious & cultural sensitivity rules

Culturally aware. Never prescriptive.

Twenty rules, split into the assumptions we refuse to make and the freedoms we guarantee. They are the cultural counterpart to the product principles — paste them into design reviews.

Never assume
  1. 01The wedding is Hindu — most South Asian weddings aren't.
  2. 02There's a "bride and groom" — allow any partner labels.
  3. 03One family structure — sides, hosts and elders vary.
  4. 04All guests attend all events — they almost never do.
  5. 05It happens on one day — assume many, across venues.
  6. 06Ceremonies follow one fixed order — they don't.
  7. 07Religious symbols are decoration — never use them carelessly.
  8. 08Users want a religious category — never force one.
  9. 09Clichés read as culture — avoid stereotyped motifs.
  10. 10Louder means more authentic — elegance over decoration.
Always allow & respect
  1. 11Custom-name every single event.
  2. 12Remove any section, page or field.
  3. 13Create entirely new, host-defined event types.
  4. 14Neutral, elegant copy by default.
  5. 15Cultural presets are optional and opt-in.
  6. 16Every religion & culture treated equally well.
  7. 17Inclusive language for couples, hosts, families, guests.
  8. 18Full support for interfaith & mixed-culture events.
  9. 19Full support for civil & secular ceremonies.
  10. 20Host controls exactly what cultural explanation appears.

Taste over options, tasteful motion, warm copy, obsessive mobile polish, and a concierge add-on that signals "white-glove." Lean is invisible when craft is high.

13 — User journeys

Two journeys carry the product.

Host journey · the buyer
  1. 01Lands on marketing, sees the template, gets it instantly
  2. 02Signs up, picks a starting point, names the couple & date
  3. 03Adds & reorders events; customises with blocks & theme
  4. 04Adds households & guests (paste or manual)
  5. 05Sets the invite matrix — who sees which event
  6. 06Sends a test, then unlocks with Stripe & publishes
  7. 07Sends invites; watches RSVPs land on the dashboard
  8. 08Reminds non-responders, publishes updates, exports final numbers
Guest journey · the volume
  1. 01Receives a personalised link by email (or WhatsApp later)
  2. 02Opens to a warm welcome with their own name

30 — Legal, trust & data

You hold guest lists. Earn the trust that implies.

Hosts are handing you their families' names, emails and dietary details. Treat that data as sacred, say so plainly, and build the protections in from day one. Privacy is also a feature you can market.

Not legal advice — a checklist to take to a solicitor / DPO before launch.

Data protection (UK/EU GDPR)
  • Lawful basis per data type (contract / consent)
  • Retention policy — auto-purge guest data N months post-event
  • One-click export & delete for hosts
  • DPAs with subprocessors (Supabase, Resend, Stripe, Vercel)
  • EU/UK data residency where possible
Guest privacy
  • Guests are not users — no accounts, no passwords
  • Access via single-use-ish secure tokens; never store raw tokens
  • Guest lists never publicly enumerable or indexable
  • Transactional email only — no marketing to guests, ever
  • Per-guest data visible only to that household
Policies to prepare
  • Privacy policy & cookie notice (analytics consent)
  • Terms of service (host responsibilities for their list)
  • Data processing addendum (you = processor for guest data)
  • Acceptable-use (anti-spam) for invite sending
  • Security page ○ — later, once you have practices to show
Support & live-event ops
  • Set support expectations clearly (hours, channel)
  • A real plan for "it's broken on the wedding day"
  • Status page / incident comms ○

The architecture is linked data, not isolated modules. A detail added once powers every surface that needs it — this is the whole product, and the reason it beats a folder of spreadsheets.

An event
entered once →
website schedule · RSVP setup · invite matrix · vendor coverage · budget · tasks · files · update feed · reports
A guest
entered once →
household · invite link · RSVP form · dietary list · attendance counts · caterer export · reminders · seating later
A vendor
entered once →
pipeline · event coverage · budget line items · payment tracking · contracts/files · linked tasks · day-of contacts
A budget item
entered once →
linked event · linked vendor · category · payment status · dashboard health · reports
Object model · plain-English relationships
Site has many Events · many Households → each has many Guests
A Guest can be invited to many Events · an Invitation = (guest × event) · an RSVP = response per event
An Event has many Vendors · a Vendor can cover many Events
A Budget Item links an Event + Vendor · a Task links Event + Vendor + Budget Item · a File attaches to any of them
A Report pulls live from connected records — never a separate copy

Final verdict

Ship the minimum competitive command centre — not website + RSVP alone.

Website-only loses to platforms that already bundle budgets and vendors. The connected loop is the moat. Keep each module simple, keep it calm, keep it easier than Excel — but ship the whole loop.
